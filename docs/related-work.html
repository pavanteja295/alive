Who else is building this

Seven outside sources, read against our own construction: a company already shipping it, four papers, and what you can clone for free. What they do, where we differ, and which ones are worth going back to.

01 / aim

What we are building

A conversational clone of a creator that sounds like them talking.

Somebody types a question. The system answers using things the creator has actually said, in the creator's own speaking voice, and then says it out loud in their cloned voice.

Three commitments that separate this from a chatbot with a persona prompt

  1. The content comes from things he actually said, retrieved with timestamps you can check, not from the base model's impression of him.
  2. Sounding like him is a separate job from knowing what he thinks, done by a separate model that never sees the question.
  3. The output is written to be spoken. Text is a waypoint, not the product.

Two halves. The left is built once per creator and is deterministic or cached. The right runs on every question.

Three stages, each gated. Inside a stage the model does what it likes; at the boundary it must pass a check. Built and running for the first stage.

Delphi

weight

Cerebras engineering blog · Pinecone customer study · VentureBeat · Sequoia podcast

A funded company selling exactly our product: a creator uploads their content and gets a chatbot that answers as them, by text and by voice. Thousands of these exist.

The public record comes in two halves that do not obviously agree, and that is the most useful thing about it.

The one disclosure that genuinely matters

The creator chooses how far the clone is allowed to go.

settingwhat it does
strictonly says things from his own material that directly answer the question
interpolateallowed to reach for material from the wider internet
extrapolatehis material only, but faced with something new, predicts what he would say

Their example: he never discussed a topic, but his principles about handling uncertainty carry across, so the clone reasons over.

We already compute this distinction. Every answer we produce carries a label saying whether the position was found, extended from nearby material, or unsupported. We report it as an output. They let the creator set it as an input. That is one step away.

Same, different, and worth taking

Delphius
matchingby meaningby shared words, and it is our measured weak spot
what is storedthe passage plus generated summaries and questionsthe passage only
answeringlook once, then writesearch repeatedly, deciding what to look for each round
speedabout a second, end to endmany round trips; nowhere near it
declininga setting the creator picksa check that is on or off
sounding like themnot disclosed anywherea separately trained model
qualityno measurement published at alla blind judge, which says ours fails

The best idea in the whole collection

Store, alongside each passage, the questions that passage would answer, and search those too. It attacks our exact measured failure: our search misses passages that plainly answer a question because the question is in the asker's words and the passage is in his. And we have already built the artifact — ten thousand such questions, generated per passage, currently used only as training data.

Three more worth having: an explicit strict-or-extrapolate setting; one sealed drawer per creator, so removing a creator is a single operation rather than a migration; and interview mode, where the clone asks its own creator questions to fill gaps. That last one is the only proposal anywhere in the collection that addresses coverage, which our own notes say prompting cannot fix.

Nice, unproven. The graph is the most attractive claim in the set — it would let a clone represent that someone changed their mind, which plain retrieval cannot. There is no published evaluation of it, no ablation, and it cuts against our decision to keep things verbatim. Believe the infrastructure; treat the graph as a hypothesis.

Verdict. The product baseline to beat, and the source of our production budget. Their retention figure — users who arrive by voice come back several times more often — is the strongest evidence in the collection that the voice end of our pipeline is the product, not the garnish.

B

Simulating a thousand people

weight

Park, Zou, Kamphorst, Egan, Shaw, Hill, Cai, Morris, Liang, Willer, Bernstein · Stanford, Northwestern, Washington, Google DeepMind · arXiv 2411.10109

They recruited a thousand Americans, ran a two-hour interview with each one, built an agent per person, and tested whether it answered surveys the way that person did.

The architecture is deliberately the simplest possible thing. No search, no database, no training. The whole interview goes into the prompt.

The idea we should steal

They do not report how often the agent was right. They report how close it got to how often the person agreed with themselves when asked the same questions a fortnight later. The ceiling is the person's own inconsistency, and it is nowhere near perfect.

That is the right shape for any task where perfect agreement is neither available nor meaningful. We already have one measure with this property: our blind style judge aims at a coin flip, not at perfection, because a coin flip means indistinguishable.

We have no equivalent on the content side, and it matters. How consistent is our creator with himself? If he answers two similar questions across two videos and says slightly different things, that spread is the real ceiling for any system answering as him — and every content number we report is currently measured against an unreachable one.

Where they and we part

Their input is a two-hour interview, elicited on purpose, about the person. Our archive is long, found rather than elicited, and about topics, with the person visible only as a side effect. That is why they can skip search and we cannot — and it is also why questions about him personally are our worst band.

The governance half

They did not release the agents. Research access only, aggregate results open, individual results behind review. They propose an audit log per agent so a participant can see what theirs is doing and withdraw consent later — permission granted one day, revocable a month after.

For a creator product that is not an ethics footnote, it is a feature list. The risks they name transfer directly: over-reliance when accuracy is low, leakage of sensitive source material, co-option of a likeness, and reputational damage from someone steering the clone into saying something it should not.

Unproven at our scale. Putting everything in the prompt works at two hours. It does not obviously work at seventeen videos, and we have already rejected whole-archive context on cost.

But this is precisely the day-one experiment our own documents specify and nobody has run: a strong model, the full transcript, no search, no training, outputs read beside his real answers. This paper is the best argument for running it, and it hands us the number to beat.

The negative replication

weight

Zichao Wang and Alexa Siu · Adobe Research · arXiv 2603.29890 · March 2026

They took the architecture above, pointed it at a different task, and it came apart. Fifty-one knowledge workers interviewed about how they work, one agent each, then both the humans and their agents rated four proposed products.

Their finding, in their words: the agents are calibrated to the crowd but imprecise about the individual. They match the population. They do not match the person.

Why this is the most important paper here for us

They measured voice and tone, and it was their worst axis. None of the agent designs adequately captured how the participants actually speak. They call the result thematically right but not experientially right.

Their description of the failure is almost word for word ours:

  • Real people ranged from enthusiasm through flat rejection to philosophical tangents. The agents were uniformly constructive — even the criticism was phrased as something a tweak could fix.
  • Real people said things like "I'm a little confused on how that works." The agents produced clear, structured interpretations every time.
  • Real people raised specific obstacles — needing sign-off, compliance rules, justifying the cost. The agents flattened these into generalities.

That is our own finding, from the other direction. Our creator breaks off and restarts mid-sentence constantly. Every model we tried — trained, prompted, small, large, local, frontier — produced none of it. Not few. None. We eliminated model size, compression, adapter size, learning rate, checkpoint choice, decoding settings, more data and more training, each by measurement.

This paper says the flattening is general, shows up in content as well as wording, and is not fixed by giving the model more evidence about the person. Independent corroboration that the thing we cannot fix is not a bug in our pipeline.

Temper the pessimism slightly. Their participants were inventing opinions on the spot about tools they had never used. Our creator has genuinely said the thing, so there is a right answer sitting in the archive. Ours may be the easier task.

The caution stands regardless. Never promise individual fidelity on the strength of population-level agreement. If we ever report "the clone sounds like him" from an average, this is the counterexample sitting in the literature waiting to be cited at us.

Bi-Mem

weight

Mao, Tan, Liu, Liu, Xu, Ji, Wang · USTC and collaborators · arXiv 2601.06490 · January 2026

The problem is not ours. They assume a long-running chat between an assistant and one user, and ask how to remember that user. We have a fixed archive of broadcast video and a creator who is not in conversation with anyone.

Their argument: build memory only from the bottom up and errors compound, so a local cluster of facts drifts away from who the person actually is. Run it both ways instead.

Read their results table carefully

They score against seven other memory systems on a shared benchmark. Two things fall out that are worth more than the method:

The whole field is fighting over a handful of points against plain search, and the winner is still well short of good. Ordinary retrieval, with none of the machinery, beats several purpose-built memory architectures. Bi-Mem's gain over it is real and statistically solid — and it costs a model call per turn to extract a fact, another per group to write a theme, more to distil a description, and another to check each theme.

Questions needing evidence from two places are where everyone is worst, by a wide margin, across all seven systems. Our own notes say our search never assembles an answer spread across two videos, and that better ranking may not be the fix. This table supports that. It moves the question from "tune our retriever" to "what shape of system composes evidence at all", which is a literature question, not an engineering one.

Worth taking anyway

  • Match on words and meaning together, blended evenly. They use it as the unremarkable default. We match on words alone, and our own measurements say that is a weak spot. This is the cheapest and least controversial upgrade in the collection, and unlike a knowledge graph it does not touch how we store anything.
  • Check the summary against the whole. Strip the extraction away and the idea survives: anything built from parts should be checked against a global view and patched where it disagrees. We build one summary per video already, and nothing checks it against anything.

Verdict. Reference implementation only. It conflicts with how we store things at the foundation. Valuable as a map of that field and as evidence about what the field cannot yet do.

What exists in the open

weight

Nothing open does the whole job. The pieces exist separately, at very different levels of seriousness.

Closest to Delphi's storage claim

Graphiti / Zep. An open graph of facts where every fact carries both the moment it became true and the moment it was superseded, so a contradicted fact is marked no-longer-true rather than deleted. You can ask what is true now, or what was true then. This is the open shape of the thing Delphi describes and will not detail.

It does not belong in the extract-and-discard group, and a first reading put it there. The raw material is kept as the ground truth and every derived fact traces back to the passage it came from. That is point-don't-replace — our own stance — and it is materially different from Bi-Mem, which summarises a turn and drops the original.

Its contradiction handling is mechanical rather than asking a model to summarise the conflict away, which avoids a failure we have hit elsewhere. And its search is word-matching and meaning-matching together with a reranking pass, at under a second: the fourth independent source saying ours is behind.

Every headline number in this category is published by the vendor whose system wins. Treat them as claims.

Closest to our whole ambition

Second Me. Runs locally, trains on your own material, on the same model family our style stage uses. The only open project that trains rather than only searches, which is the half of our system nobody else attempts.

Read properly, it comes apart in ways that matter, and one part of it is worth taking anyway. Its own entry is below.

The memory layer, commodity

Mem0, Letta, Cognee. Bolt onto an existing agent. Widely adopted. All of them extract facts and drop the original.

The role-play family, academic

Training models to be a character is nominally our stage B. Two reasons it may not transfer: they target fictional or historical figures where there is no ground truth to contradict, and they are scored on staying in character rather than on being mistaken for a specific living person.

Below that sits a tier of small projects doing "chat with a YouTube channel" with meaning-based search and citations. Useful only as confirmation that the search half of our stage A is commodity, and that what actually differentiates us is the parts nobody has built.

Raw beats summarised

weight

Leonard Kinzinger and Jochen Hartmann · TU Munich · arXiv 2606.04592 · June 2026

Everyone else here builds a twin from material collected in order to build a twin. These authors point out that no company has that. What companies have is years of accumulated, uneven records gathered for other reasons. So they built twins from a long-running household survey never designed for the purpose.

Then they did something the rest of the collection does not. Instead of building one system and reporting how well it did, they built the same twin many different ways and compared the ways against each other — several models, several amounts of information, two ways of handing it over, with and without letting the model think first. Every combination, scored on the same held-out questions from the same people.

They say plainly why. The summary is good prose, but compressing a scale answer into a sentence collapses a precise value into an approximate description. Their own example: a person picked the very lowest of six options, a flat "does not apply at all", and the summary rendered it as a soft "she does not really". The summary is fluent, shorter, cheaper, and it has quietly destroyed the thing the twin needed.

This is the strongest evidence anywhere in the collection for our decision to keep his words and never extract from them. It is a direct head-to-head between the two options, which nobody else in this batch runs, and the raw side wins outright. Bi-Mem builds its entire hierarchy on the assumption that the distilled version is the useful one; this paper tests that assumption and finds against it.

The mechanism they name is ours, arrived at independently. We say a summary of what he said cannot be spoken in his voice. They say a summary of what she answered cannot reproduce her answer. Same loss, two different consequences of it.

Two more findings worth holding

  • Raw costs several times more to hand over, which is the honest trade and the reason people reach for summaries. Our shape — keep it raw, but fetch only the part you need — is a third option neither of them tested.
  • More material about the person helps, then flattens out well before you have used all of it. Worth knowing before assuming more videos is automatically better.
  • Letting the model think first sharpened who-resembles-whom without making any individual answer more accurate. A useful warning: extra reasoning can improve a ranking without improving a single output.

Where it does not transfer. Their task is predicting which box a person ticks. Ours is producing prose in that person's voice. A twin that picks the right option has not demonstrated it can say anything. And their "raw" is still curated question-and-answer pairs, not seventeen hours of someone talking about other things.

Verdict. Adopt as supporting evidence. It does not change our architecture; it defends one we already chose, against the one paper here that assumes the opposite.

G

Sounding right is not the same as passing

weight

Rebira Jemama and Rajesh Kumar · Bucknell University · arXiv 2509.24930 · September 2025

The first paper in the collection whose entire subject is our stage B. Not "does it know what the person thinks" but "does it write like them", asked directly.

They build a cheap detector of whether two pieces of text share an author, then use it to score five models imitating a writer under four different setups.

Three findings, in descending order of use to us

1   How you ask beats which model you use

Describing the style in words achieved almost nothing, across all five models. Showing one real sample of the author's writing transformed the result. Two long samples was better again. And best of all was not asking at all: give the model the first half of something the author genuinely wrote and let it carry on. Under that setup the detector essentially could not tell the continuation from the original. The largest model tested was not the best.

Same shape as our own result that prompt wording bought us almost nothing while search strategy bought a great deal: the lever is the setup, not the size. And it is actionable — our style stage is a trained rewriter that instructs. The strongest baseline in this paper is one we have never tried: establish the voice with real passages and continue.

2   Sounding like the person and being obviously machine-written are separate problems

Even when their detector was fully convinced the imitation was the real author, the text was still markedly smoother and more predictable than human writing. Human text is lumpy: it takes odd turns, it is uneven, it surprises. Machine text imitating it is smooth, even when every surface habit has been copied. And none of the four setups changed this. Better imitation did not make the output less predictable.

That is our zero-disfluency finding, measured from a completely different direction — a different task, in writing rather than speech. We had ruled out every cheap cause by measurement and were left with an untested hypothesis: a model trained to produce the most likely next word will never produce the unpredictable bit, because the unpredictable bit is by definition not the most likely. This paper measures exactly that gap, names it as a separate axis, and shows it does not close as fidelity improves. It moves our hypothesis from plausible to supported from outside.

3   Same person, different setting, reads as a stranger

Their detector broke down in a specific and telling way when comparing an author's formal writing against their casual writing. Same human, and it repeatedly called them strangers. The authors name this as the limiting factor for real deployment.

This is our situation by construction. Our targets are automatic transcripts of someone performing to a camera. Our inputs are a model's tidy prose. That is a register gap before any style question is asked.

The large caveat, and it is ours to notice

Their detector is the same kind of instrument our own work already caught being wrong. It scores by letter patterns and meaning embeddings. We measured a letter-pattern style score giving a flawless result to an output that was one word repeated fifty times — because repeating the creator's filler is maximally his pattern. Our blind judge, reading actual text, caught what the pattern score could not.

This paper has no human judge anywhere in it. So its striking result, that continuation produces text the detector cannot distinguish from the author, reads as the detector cannot tell, which is not the same claim as a person cannot tell. Their own predictability finding is arguably the evidence that something is still off, which makes the paper quietly self-undermining in a useful way.

It is also written work, not speech, at a small venue. Take the ideas; do not take it as a settled instrument.

What it does for our unresolved measurement question

Our two internal documents disagree about what to measure style with. One says use the standard authorship models everyone publishes against; the other says those do not survive speech and topic separation, and prefers counting concrete habits.

This paper lands on the second side, offers a cheap protocol that needs no training, and adds a third axis neither of ours has: predictability, measured independently of style fidelity.

That third axis is new to us, and it is not gameable the way the others are. An output that repeats one word fifty times is maximally predictable, so it would be caught rather than rewarded. It also runs cheaply, which matters a great deal given that our current style measurement is slow enough to foreclose a search before it starts.

Second Me

weight

Wei, Ying, Gao, Bao, Tao, Shang · Mindverse.ai and UC San Diego · arXiv 2503.08102 · March 2025 · code Apache-2.0

The only open project in the collection that trains a model on a person rather than only searching their material. That is the half of our system nobody else attempts, which is why it was worth reading properly.

It is a personal assistant that represents you to other services: answering questions from your own history, filling in what you would have said when you query something else, and criticising what an external system sends back. Three layers of memory — your raw material, summaries of it in plain language, and at the top memories held in the model's weights rather than in words at all.

The reason to read it, and the reason not to adopt it, are both in one picture.

Why not to adopt it

  • The project is abandoned. Last real code change May 2025, one documentation edit that September, nothing since. Sixteen months, and over a hundred open issues. Fifteen thousand stars is launch-week attention, not use. A dead repository with fifteen thousand stars is a worse bet than a live one with five hundred.
  • The evaluation eats its own tail. The training examples are generated by a model. The test questions are generated by the same pipeline, held apart from the training set but manufactured identically. The answers are scored by a model against a written rubric. At no point does anything touch what a person actually said or chose.
  • They admit the judge rewards length, and their own showcase example is an instance of it. The paper notes that the scoring model "favors longer responses, particularly in metrics like Completeness and Empathy" — then presents, as the winning output, a passage markedly longer and denser with terminology than the alternative it beat. This is the failure we hit ourselves, where a measure rated a bad output above a good one.
  • And the score sits at its ceiling. Their human check lands at 0.95 and "close to 1". A measure pinned at the top cannot tell "barely moved" from "did not move", so it cannot steer anything — the rule we already hold ourselves to.
  • Every number comes from one person, and that person works there. The sample size is stated, in an appendix: the results are those of "an internal staff member who has agreed to make their test results public", with 132 notes and 62 to-do items. Two hundred and forty test items, one employee. Set that against a thousand strangers with held-out answers they really gave.
  • No outside comparison. Every table compares their own variants with each other. Nothing against a plain model, or against search alone.
  • They argue their own measure is too harsh on them — that the real quality exceeds what the scores show. An unusual thing for a paper to claim about itself.
  • The memory layers are the summarise-and-discard pattern, with weights at the top. Same shape as Bi-Mem, and it cuts against the storage decision that now has outside evidence behind it.
  • The task is not ours. No style evaluation anywhere in it. No voice. It represents you to software, not to a listener.
  • The applications section is a pitch. It claims that joining human and machine nodes raises network efficiency by three to five orders of magnitude, with no source, and proposes a token-based marketplace for personal cognitive assets. Worth knowing before weighting anything else in the paper.

The exception, and it is not small

They trained on preferences, not only on examples, and it helped on every measure they report. About a fifth of their training material was pairs of the form this answer over that one rather than copy this answer.

That matters for one specific reason. Preference training is not next-word prediction. It does not carry the property that deletes an optional, unpredictable word — which is the property that survived every elimination we ran on disfluency, and the route the style literature independently names as the answer. Their base model and fine-tuning method are the same family and the same technique as our own style stage, so the shape of the recipe transfers even though none of the results do.

Read one section: how they build preference pairs out of one person's own material. Skip the rest and do not build on the code.

Carry this caveat with it. Their improvement is measured on the circular score, so this is evidence the pipeline is buildable, not evidence it works. A worked example, not a result.

Downgraded. A first pass rated this worth a proper look, on the strength of being the only open project that trains rather than searches. That claim survives and means much less than it sounded.

Credit where it is due: the limitations section says it plainly — "large-scale evaluation is constrained by limited real-world user feedback". The paper does not hide what it could not do. Read front to back including appendices, 2026-09-15.

12 / set

What the collection says as a set

Six things that only appear when the sources are put side by side.

1

Nobody has solved sounding like the person, and only two even measured it

Delphi does not disclose how it handles voice. Park and colleagues do not model it, because forced-choice survey answers have no voice. Bi-Mem does not touch it. The role-play literature scores staying in character, not being a specific living person.

Two measured it, and both came back negative. One found voice and tone was their worst axis across every design they tried, and described the failure as flattening. The other got imitation good enough to fool its own detector, then showed the output was still far smoother than human writing, with nothing closing the gap.

Two negative results from opposite directions, and they agree: the surface can be copied and something underneath still does not come across. That is the opportunity, and it is why our measurement problem has no off-the-shelf answer. We are not failing to find the standard instrument. There is not one.

2

Everyone who measures honestly normalises against the person's own inconsistency

One reports how close the agent got to how well the person agreed with themselves a fortnight later. The other reports the same-human-twice comparison as the ceiling row of its own results table. Both refuse to treat perfection as the target, because a person does not reach it against themselves.

We already do this on one axis: our blind style judge aims at a coin flip. We do it nowhere on the content side, and we have no idea what our creator's own self-consistency is. Every content number we have is measured against an unreachable ceiling.

3

The storage question splits the field, and one paper now settles it our way

Bi-Mem extracts facts and builds a description. Mem0 extracts. Letta extracts. Delphi's founder describes a graph, which is extraction. Park and colleagues dodge it by storing nothing at all. Almost everyone assumes the distilled version is the useful one.

Kinzinger and Hartmann actually tested it, and the raw material won in every single configuration. The reason they give is the reason we gave: compressing an answer into good prose quietly replaces a precise thing with an approximate one.

That changes the standing of our decision. It was a position argued from a consequence nobody else has to care about — that a summary cannot be spoken in the person's voice. It now also has outside evidence behind it on a task where voice is irrelevant, which is a stronger place to stand. If we ever loosen it, this is the paper that has to be answered.

4

Composing evidence across sources is unsolved everywhere

Our notes say one search never returns a complete answer spread across two videos. Bi-Mem's table shows the same failure, by a wide margin, for the best system in a field of seven purpose-built architectures.

This is not a defect of our retriever. It is where the field is. It moves the question from fixing our ranking to asking what shape of system assembles composed evidence at all.

5

Production is a one-second budget and we are not built for it

Delphi hits about a second end to end, with search taking well under a third of it, by looking once and then writing. Our stage A makes several sequential model calls just to decide what to search for, then another to answer, then hands off to a local style model, then to a voice service.

This is a research loop, not a serving path. Worth stating as a known property rather than discovering later. The cheerful version of the same point: their retention figure says voice is worth building toward, not treating as polish.

6

Predictability is a separate axis, and nobody here was looking for it

It falls out sideways. They set out to measure whether imitation works, found it does by their own instrument, and noticed on the way that the output stayed markedly smoother than the human writing it copied, however good the imitation got.

Set that beside the flattening finding, and beside our creator stammering while every model we tried produced none of it, and the three are plausibly one phenomenon seen three ways. A model asked for the most likely continuation will not produce the parts of a person that were not likely.

If that holds it is a property of the objective, not a defect to be fixed with better data or a bigger model — both of which we have already ruled out by measurement. And it suggests an instrument we do not have: cheap, needing no human, and one that a degenerate repeating output fails rather than wins.

What this reading suggests we do not know

None of this has been written into our register. A person decides what is real.

already in the register, now with outside support

16 / stages

Naming the stages, and what we cannot yet answer about them

A first attempt at cutting the pipeline into stages that can be argued about separately. Not yet qualified — a stage is only real when breaking it moves a measurement and points back at it, and that test has not been run here.

The first correction the attempt produced. What feels like three stages — holding his persona, searching, writing the answer — is one. They share a single prompt and happen inside the same calls, with no artifact passing between them. So they cannot be measured or swapped independently. That is a finding about the seams being too coarse, not a tidying-up.

Two things found while checking these questions

The per-video description is used nowhere when the system actually runs. The server never switches it on, and the one path that would use it — saying what he does cover when declining — sits inside a branch the server also never enables. It costs a model call per video to build and is dead in the deployed path.

Our own status note about the passages is stale. It records them as automatically picked and the curation as untested. On disk there are eighteen, hand-curated, and a scored experiment named after exactly that. The work was done and the status line never caught up — which is the failure mode our own working rules warn about.

The questions, and whether the reading answers them

Three marks: answered by work in this collection, partly answered, or open — meaning nobody here has addressed it and we have not measured it either.

Why cut into fixed overlapping pieces? What was the hypothesis? answered There was no hypothesis of ours. It came from a paper finding fixed pieces beat meaning-based ones on retrieval, evidence and generation, and was filed as settled and creator-independent. Never tested on our data.
What is the per-video description for? Is it a higher-level search? answered Intended as a bridge from the asker's words to his. Measured in front of the searcher and rejected: questions about him personally regressed badly, because it indexes topics and says nothing about the man. Now used nowhere at run time.
Are the generated questions used for anything but style training? answered No. A separate small hand-banded set with ground-truth labels drives evaluation. The ten thousand generated ones only mint style pairs. Delphi indexes exactly this artifact for search and we do not.

How does the search work, and why is it forced to happen first? answered Word overlap weighted toward rare words, top six, plus one neighbouring piece either side. Searching first is enforced in code, not requested: left free, the model skips the search on topics it assumes are outside his world and answers from its own knowledge, the exact failure this system exists to prevent.
How many turns, and how does it know to answer like him? answered Two to six model calls: up to five rounds of its own searches, then one to write. It knows from three things in one prompt — the identity rules, the eighteen passages, and the length and audience directive.
Would an agent with a harness, looping and tool calls be better? answered It already is one, and it is the biggest measured win in the system: letting the model write its own searches was worth roughly forty times better prompt wording, in a design where the two cannot be confused. Everyone else in this collection looks once. Untried: more than one tool, a critique pass, and offering the video descriptions as a tool it may call rather than text pushed at it.
What share of the job does the style stage own? partly Smaller than it looks. With no archive at all the bare model already produced fluent, correctly-pitched answers with none of his content: it had the voice and not the content. The residual is narrow in counts — a handful of spoken habits he uses several times more often — and yet total in effect, since the blind judge is never once fooled. That asymmetry is unexplained and nobody outside has measured it.
How do we get speech disfluencies? partly Everything cheap is eliminated by measurement. Reading adds that this is general, not ours, and does not close as imitation improves. Four live ideas below.
Should disfluencies be added by the big model or the style model? answered Settled negatively for the big model. A frontier model shown six real transcripts and told plainly that he breaks off and restarts produced none. So it is not a question of a bigger or better model. It points at a different objective, or at not learning them at all.
Can the style model carry disfluencies plus the remaining corrections? open There is a conflict to name first. It is trained only on pairs a judge confirmed say the same thing — explicitly trained to change nothing but voice. Disfluency adds material. One model doing both means teaching it to add some things and not others, which is the distinction the pair judge exists to remove. Either two stages, or the criterion changes on purpose.

Four live ideas for disfluency, cheapest first

  1. Ask a model to copy a stammered passage word for word. One afternoon. If it cannot even copy one, this is a refusal learned in instruction training, not an inability — and those are the two hypotheses currently tied.
  2. Do not learn them at all; add them afterwards, sampled from his own measured rate and positions. Sidesteps the averaging problem entirely. The risk is that placement is what makes a stammer read as real.
  3. Train against a preference signal rather than next-word likelihood. The literature names this as the answer for style, and it does not have the property that makes an optional, unpredictable word disappear. Second Me is a worked example of this on the same base model family and the same fine-tuning method as ours, which makes it the cheapest available template even though its own results cannot be believed.
  4. Treat them as marks to control rather than text to learn, as spontaneous-speech synthesis does with explicit tags.

A caveat from our own data: part of what reads as disfluency is transcription error, not speech. One tell the judge picked up on was a mis-transcribed word. So not all of the gap is desirable to close, and deciding which part is real comes before deciding how to produce it.

18 / where

Where disfluency lives, which nobody has decided

He breaks off and restarts constantly. Every model we have tried — trained, prompted, small, large, local, frontier — produces none of it. We have eliminated model size, compression, adapter size, learning rate, checkpoint, decoding settings, repetition penalty, more data and more training, each by measurement.

Checking whether speech markup could help turned up the more useful question. There are three places this could live, and we have only ever tried one.

What speech markup can and cannot do

Markup for speech synthesis lets you control timing, pitch, rate, loudness, emphasis and pronunciation. You can insert a pause of an exact length, stretch a phrase, lift a syllable.

There is no element that inserts "um", and none that repeats a word. Filled pauses and restarts are words; they have to be in the text. So markup cannot produce his signature disfluency. It can carry the pauses around it, which we currently lose completely — a real gain, but a different one.

Our own provider has also moved. Their newest model dropped speech markup entirely and replaced it with bracketed cues. Older models keep it. We have pinned no model anywhere in the tree, so which family we use is still a decision rather than a constraint we inherited.

Why the tag idea is still right, just not as markup

Our open explanation for the zero is that a model trained to pick the most likely next word will delete an optional filler, because a filler whose position is unpredictable is never the most likely word.

A tag changes the competition. If the decision at a position is "marker or no marker", that is a small choice in a dedicated slot rather than a fight against the whole vocabulary. This is the same idea as the spontaneous-speech synthesis work sitting unread in our register, which tags disfluencies as symbols for control — arrived at independently from the other end, which is worth noting.

The finding that reframes the problem

A voice cloned from audio containing hesitations may produce them itself, during pauses, the way a real speaker does. The provider states this. If it holds, disfluency need not be produced by any text stage at all. It lives in the voice, which is where it naturally belongs, because disfluency is a property of speech and not of writing.

Two consequences worth taking seriously.

Our own notes already record that the rhythm and melody of his speech are lost where the pipeline narrows to text, twice over, and file it as deliberate. Disfluency is plausibly the same category — a speech phenomenon we have been forcing through a text bottleneck. We never questioned that framing because the style stage looked like the obvious owner.

And the number we are chasing may be wrong. His stammer rate was measured off automatic transcripts, so it records what the transcriber chose to write down, not what he did. The true rate in the audio is almost certainly higher — and a cloned voice would learn from the audio, not from the transcript.

This is a decision, not an exploration

Our own working rules sort unresolved things four ways, and only one of them gets a search. Which stage owns disfluency is a decision a person settles, and it bounds everything downstream: what the style model is being asked to learn, whether its content-preserving criterion has to change, and which voice model we commit to.

It is currently filed as an exploration. That may be why it has absorbed a great deal of measurement without closing: we have been searching inside a choice nobody made.

19 / thesis

Why this should produce a better model

The argument for the two bets, stated so it can be attacked.

The problem, in one sentence

A model produces its most likely output. A person is not their most likely output.

Three findings in this collection are the same observation from three directions. Agents flatten specific obstacles into generalities and authentic confusion into clean interpretation. Imitations stay about half as unpredictable as the human writing they copy, however convincing they read. And our creator stammers while every model we tried, at every size, trained or prompted, produced exactly none.

Those are not three bugs. They are one property. The gap between a model and a person is the gap between the average and the particular — and it belongs to the objective, not to the scale. More parameters make the average better, not more particular. That is why a larger model did not beat a smaller one on our task, and why a frontier model shown six real transcripts still produced nothing.

Why loops, and why the metric is the load-bearing part

Everything the field does puts the person into the prompt as evidence: a two-hour interview, retrieved passages, a description of how they talk. Both papers here show that adding more evidence reaches a ceiling and does not fix the flattening.

It cannot, and the reason is structural. Evidence conditions the distribution. The averaging happens afterwards, when the model picks from it. So no quantity of evidence changes what the model does with what it has.

A measurement is different in kind. It turns the person from evidence into a target — and a loop is the only structure that can act on a target. A single pass has no mechanism for "that came out too smooth, go rougher", because it cannot see what it produced. You cannot prompt your way out of averaging, because prompting happens before the average is taken.

Which is why the metric, not the loop, is the thing being invested in. The loop is just the mechanism that lets a measurement act. Everything rests on the measurement being right, and a loop pointed at the wrong measurement walks away from him faster than no loop at all.

Why small intermediate models, for a reason beyond cost

The obvious argument is economic. A loop needs cheap measurement; at twenty-five minutes a reading you get two iterations, not a search. Small models make the meters free, and that is what turns a handful of guesses into an actual search. One-shot the components so the system can afford to loop.

The deeper argument is that factoring a decision is what makes the unlikely reachable.

A stammer has to beat every other word in the language at that position. It never does, and raising the randomness to let it through wrecks every other word too. But a model choosing among four things — nothing, a filled pause, a repeat, a restart — can be sampled freely, because the worst case is a stumble slightly misplaced rather than a nonsense sentence.

Same principle, stated generally: a rare, optional, human-particular event is unreachable inside one enormous joint decision and reachable inside a small factored one. That is not a workaround for disfluency. It is the general shape of how anything specific about a person survives a model that is built to average.

With one constraint the evidence already gives us: distil decisions, not prose. Choosing, gating, ranking and placing all shrink well. Writing a paragraph faithfully does not — we measured a very small model at roughly a tenth of the faithfulness it needed.

How inference-time work becomes a better model

The loop does not only improve one answer. Every accepted draft, and every candidate it beat, is a preference pair. That is the training signal nobody in this field has for this task — the one thing that would let us train against something other than next-word likelihood, which is the objective that causes the problem in the first place.

So it compounds: the harness produces ranked candidates, the candidates train the model, the better model needs fewer iterations, and the freed budget buys more search. The loop is the data generator, not just the runtime.

Why this bet is available to us and not to them

Everyone shipping this is pinned to about a second per answer, because they are products. Spending a minute on one reply is not a design they can evaluate.

We are not a product. The region of the design space where you spend real compute per answer is unexplored by the people with the most resources, for reasons that have nothing to do with whether it works. That is an asymmetry an open project can take.

Where the argument breaks, stated by us rather than found later

It has one point of failure and it is the metric. A loop amplifies whatever it measures, and distillation makes that permanent — a loop you can inspect becomes weights you cannot. Every claim above is downstream of instruments we have not yet built and, on the style axis, cannot yet agree on.

And none of it is tested here. We have never shown that revision beats a single pass on this task. Both papers in this collection describe iteration converging toward blandness, which is the same direction we would be pushing against. That control comes before anything else gets built.

Stated plainly so that it is the first thing attacked, rather than the last thing discovered.
